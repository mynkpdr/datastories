TDS Student Intelligence Brief · Jan 2025 – May 2026

How should
we intervene?

Across four terms and 4,466 known students, Tools in Data Science has generated one of the richest behavioral datasets in IITM's online program. Students leave traces everywhere — how many times they save an answer, whether they ever post a question, whether they collapse under a timed exam or flourish across weeks of iteration.

Those traces tell us something important: students do not all need the same kind of help. Some are silent and struggling — they never ask for help and barely attempt assessments. Some are working incredibly hard but have never learned to manage time-boxed pressure. Some are already brilliant community builders who could become the program's most valuable resource.

This brief answers the question Anand posed: What are the 5–10 intervention strategies, and which students belong to each? Every section is grounded in observed behavior across 276,562 submissions and 11,868 forum posts, not assumptions.

Chapter 00 · Overview

The classroom in numbers.

Before we can intervene, we need to see who is actually in the room. The numbers below are not abstract — they represent 4,466 individual learning journeys, most of them undocumented, many of them struggling silently. The headline finding is that 65% of students never posted a single forum message, even though the forum had a median first reply time of just 84 minutes. Silence is not just a proxy for disengagement; it's a measurable risk factor, with silent students averaging 12 fewer percentage points on their final scores than active forum participants.

Assessment gain: first save → last save
When students can iterate over days or weeks, they improve dramatically. The ROE's 45-minute window compresses this loop — and the scores show it. Students who submit GAs multiple times average a 42-point improvement. ROE improvement is just 13 points — under time pressure, iteration barely helps. This shows the importance of providing students with adequate time to iterate and improve their work.
Forum engagement vs average score
Three engagement cohorts emerge naturally from the data. Students who post actively (10+ times) score 18.6 points higher on average than students who never post. This is a striking correlation — though not necessarily causation. Active engagement may signal deeper motivation that also drives higher scores. Either way, silence is a signal worth acting on.
Chapter 01 · The Intervention Map

Five profiles.
Five strategies.

Every student in TDS can be placed into one of five behavioral profiles based on observable signals: forum activity, attempt count, latest score, and ROE performance. These are not character judgments — they are operational labels that map directly to intervention types. A student in the "Silent Risk" profile needs a completely different kind of support than an "ROE Struggler," even if both have similar final grades.

These profiles cover the vast majority of the 4,466 known students. Some students will span multiple profiles — the data surfaces the dominant pattern. Importantly, the "Forum Helper" cohort (178 students) is an asset, not just a beneficiary — this group should be actively recruited as Learning Assistants and community builders, as Anand suggested.

Together, these five profiles suggest 5 primary intervention strategies, with 2–3 additional sub-strategies for edge cases discussed in the Actions section. None of these require new infrastructure — they require knowing who is who, and reaching them before silence becomes failure.

Chapter 02 · Assessment Behavior

The course works — except under time pressure.

TDS's open-save model is genuinely powerful. Students who make 10 or more GA attempts average an 83% final score — 26 points higher than those who submit only once. Projects show a similar pattern. This is not gaming the system; it is learning through feedback, which is exactly what the course intends. The average student saves a GA first answer at 32% and improves it to 74% — a 42-point gain through iteration alone.

The Remote Online Exam breaks this pattern entirely. With only 45 minutes to work, the median first-to-last save span collapses from hours (for GAs) to minutes. Across all terms, GA scores average 73–77%. ROE scores have been falling: 56% in Jan 2025, then 29%, 44%, and an alarming 13% in Jan 2026. The ROE gap in Jan 2026 was 63.5 percentage points between GA performance and ROE performance — the widest it has ever been.

This tells us two things about intervention. First, the course's feedback loop works and should be preserved. Second, students need dedicated ROE preparation — timed drills, environment setup practice, and a mindset shift from the iterative GA workflow to time-boxed sprint execution.

Students who attempt GAs more times achieve dramatically higher scores. The gap between one attempt (57%) and ten-plus attempts (83%) is 26 points. Iteration is not cheating — it is the intended learning mechanism.

Chapter 03 · Forum Signals

The forum is fast, concentrated, and predictive.

With 11,868 posts, 848 topics, and 1,595 participants across all terms, TDS Discourse is an unusually active course forum. The median first reply comes in just 84 minutes — one of the fastest response times in any IITM course. Staff account for 20.9% of posts, meaning roughly 4 of every 5 posts are student-to-student conversation. This is a genuine peer learning network, not just a help desk.

But activity is highly concentrated. The top 16 discussion threads account for 49% of all posts. This concentration has a practical implication: staffing those key threads well — pinning canonical answers, assigning TAs to monitor high-traffic topics — could improve the experience for the majority of engaged students without creating new infrastructure.

More importantly: the 2,915 students who never posted are not necessarily disengaged — some may be lurkers who read but don't write. However, behavioral data shows that students who post in a quiz's official thread score 23–30 points higher on that quiz than students who don't. This is not a small effect. The practical question is: how do we draw silent students into the conversation before they fall behind?

Monthly forum activity
Post volume tracks the academic calendar closely — spikes align with GA and Project deadlines. Activity drops sharply in the months between terms (Aug 2025, Jan 2026), which are opportunities to reach out to incoming students before the pressure begins.
Highest-traffic discussion threads
Project threads dominate. The top 5 threads alone account for 3,213 posts. GA2 (Deployment Tools) consistently generates the highest per-student forum traffic and shows the largest score gap between posters and non-posters — suggesting it's the course's hardest operational bottleneck.
Score gap: students who posted in quiz threads vs. those who didn't
For each quiz that had an official discussion thread, we can compare scores between students who posted at least once vs. those who didn't. The gap is consistent across all quizzes — students who engage with the thread score measurably higher. This is the single most actionable finding in the forum data: getting silent students to participate in discussion threads could meaningfully improve their outcomes.
Chapter 04 · Course Team

The human layer behind the forum.

The forum is not just a peer network. It is held together by a small human support layer: faculty, instructors, TAs, and Learning Assistants who turn scattered questions into a functioning classroom. Across the four main terms, this group produced 2,728 support posts — 23% of all term-tagged Discourse activity analyzed here.

The average first course-team reply to a student-started topic is 28.0 hours, but the median is only 4.1 hours. That gap matters: most questions receive fast attention, while a long tail of unresolved or slow-moving topics pulls the average upward. Direct replies are even faster, with a 1.9-hour median.

Jan 2026 is the strongest term operationally: coverage rises to 73.9% of student-started topics and the median first course-team reply drops to 2.7 hours.

Chapter 05 · Timing

When students work.
When students ask.

The timing data turns the intervention question into an operations question. Students do not simply submit late; they cluster work into a few intense weekend and night windows, with a separate ROE spike around 13:00 IST because the exam runs in a roughly 45-minute window. The forum follows the same rhythm, which means support, pinned answers, and validator reliability matter most at predictable moments.

The strongest timing signal is the final-save gap: GA students who finish 3+ days early average 85.7%, while last-6-hour finishers average 70.7%. Projects show the same pattern. This does not prove early saving causes success, but it does show where a small behavioral nudge could move outcomes.

Chapter 06 · Early Warning

Silence in week one
predicts failure by week twelve.

The most urgent insight in this dataset is that at-risk students can be identified early — not retrospectively. Three signals, visible within the first two weeks of a term, strongly predict who will end up in the "Silent Risk" category by the end: no forum posts, fewer than 3 submissions on GA1, and a GA1 score below 40%.

Students meeting all three criteria in week one are nearly certain to underperform the entire term. The 848 students currently labeled "Silent Risk" averaged just 30.3% overall — compared to 73% for actively engaged students. Yet as Anand noted, the intervention needs to happen at the start, not at the end. By the time we notice these students are missing, the term is already half over.

The recommendation is to build a simple weekly flag system: any student who has zero forum activity AND fewer than 3 saves on their first GA receives an automated nudge in week 2. Triggered early enough, this flag costs almost nothing. Triggered after week 6, it's largely too late to shift outcomes. The goal is not to identify who failed — it's to find who is about to fail, while there's still time to help.

Deadline behavior: how close to the wire do students submit?
Late behavior is instructive. For GAs, 47% of all final saves happen in the last 6 hours before deadline. For projects, the pattern is healthier: more students save early and iterate over days. The ROE is exceptional — it is literally a 45-minute window, so all saves are by definition last-minute. The "Late Sprinter" archetype (3,174 students) represents this deadline-cramming pattern at scale: they are not necessarily lazy, they are under-informed about how the feedback loop works. A "save early, improve often" nudge campaign targeted at GA1 week could shift many Late Sprinters toward the Iterative Grinder behavior — without requiring any change to the course structure itself.
Conclusion · Action Plan

Seven moves.
Two tracks.

Anand proposed two tracks: Track 1 — how do we identify the right intervention for students in the next term? Track 2 — how do we support alumni who have already completed TDS? The data supports concrete answers to both. Track 1 focuses on early detection and targeted intervention during the term. Track 2 is about leveraging the Forum Helper and Iterative Grinder cohorts as bridges — connecting current students with alumni who succeeded through similar challenges.

The seven recommendations below are ranked by priority and mapped to owners. They are not aspirational — every one of them is actionable using tools that already exist. None require new technology; they require using data the platform already captures to make timely, targeted decisions.

Appendix · Methodology

How to read this data.

These findings are descriptive associations, not causal claims. A student who posts on the forum is correlated with higher scores — but posting does not cause higher scores, and silent students are not definitively less capable. The archetypes here are operational labels designed to guide intervention design, not character judgments. Every archetype has exceptions, and every intervention should be offered, not imposed.
